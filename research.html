Soft matter · Interfacial science · Self-assembly

While emulsion droplets can, in principle, be written, erased, and rewritten or transformed into distinct morphologies, achieving rapid and reversible switching remains challenging because long-lived structures are stabilized by deep free-energy minima and thus are slow to reconfigure. This trade-off motivates the need for design principles that control inter-droplet interactions to create soft materials capable of rapidly switching between multiple long-lived states.

We report a biphasic liquid system that permits rapid and reversible transformation between multiple long-lived ordered states under transient electric fields, with each state mediated by elastic interactions of nematic liquid crystals (in preparation). We examine how coupled dielectric and capillary instabilities generate droplet arrays with uniformly oriented topological dipoles, and how elastic (induced) dipolar interactions drive the reorganization of droplets into lattice structures with various symmetries.

keywords: reconfigurable soft materials, dielectric and capillary instabilities, colloidal self-assembly

Rapid and reversible shape transformations in liquids have been difficult to realize because physical properties such as low interfacial tension and viscosity often lead to fast relaxation of the liquids. I contributed to the discovery and understanding of shape-shifting microdomains in a biphasic liquid system comprising isotropic oils and a liquid crystalline oil (Nature 2025). Our system can rapidly switch between wetting-film and emulsified states, with potential applications in tunable optical metamaterials.

keywords: soft matter physics, electrohydrodynamics, emulsification

Fluid (air-liquid or liquid-liquid) interfaces, especially those with high interfacial tension, can damage mammalian cells. However, the cell-scale processes that underlie their damage and disassembly at quiescent fluid interfaces have not been fully elucidated. I discovered that interactions of mammalian cells with thin films of nematic liquid crystals generate spatiotemporal optical signatures that yield insight into the pathways by which single cells are damaged at fluid interfaces.

The optical patterns reflect the role of interfacial tension and elasticity in cell spreading as well as the identity of cellular components. I still recall the excitement of first observing the starburst-like optical domains at the interfaces.

keywords: spreading phenomena, Marangoni fingering instability, capillary fracturing, glycocalyx
